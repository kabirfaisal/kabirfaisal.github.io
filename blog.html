Part 1

My paper summaries

Each summary follows the same shape — the problem, what I did, how it was judged, what came out, and why it matters. Full citations are on the publications page.

These five papers form one line of work. The 2024 book chapter showed that deep learning can cope with the tangled, mixed-type data of emergency care. The imputation study and WLSTM-GAN then addressed two things that block such models in practice — missing values and too little shareable data. The TeleNotArzt assistant brings it together into a working diagnosis-and-treatment recommender, and the synthetic-data classification study measures how much the generated data actually helps.

2026Springer LNCSSynthetic data

Using synthetic data to improve disease classification

Enhancing Disease Classification in Emergency Medical Scenarios Through Synthetic Data: A Comparative Study — Kabir, Nayem & Tomforde.

Does adding computer-generated patient records actually make a diagnosis classifier better — and by how much?

The problem. A model that predicts a patient’s condition learns from labelled examples. In emergency medicine those are hard to come by: datasets are small, cannot be shared for privacy reasons, and are dominated by a few common presentations while the conditions that most need reliable detection are rare. Trained on data like that, a classifier becomes good at the common cases and unreliable exactly where it matters.

What I did. I took a real emergency dataset, generated additional synthetic patient records with several different generative models, and mixed those into the training data in controlled proportions. Then I re-trained the same classifier under each setting and compared the results — paying particular attention to the rare, under-represented conditions rather than just the overall average.

What came out. Synthetic data can give a real improvement, especially for the minority classes, but it is not a free win: the size of the gain depends heavily on which generator is used and how much synthetic data is blended in. Some combinations helped little or slightly hurt. The value is in choosing the method deliberately, not in adding synthetic rows for their own sake.

Why it matters. It is a practical way to strengthen emergency-care models without waiting years for more real cases or exposing patient data — and it shows which generation approach is worth the effort.

In short: synthetic patients help a diagnosis classifier — if you pick the right generator.

Reference: Kabir, M. F., Nayem, M. M. I., & Tomforde, S. (2026). Enhancing disease classification in emergency medical scenarios through synthetic data: a comparative study. In Agents and Artificial Intelligence, Lecture Notes in Computer Science, Springer Nature Switzerland, pp. 286–308. DOI: 10.1007/978-3-032-25035-3_14.

2026ICAARTCore PhD work

A diagnosis & treatment assistant for TeleNotArzt care

Diagnosis and Treatment Support as an AI-Driven Assistant System for TeleNotary Emergency Care — Kabir, Piliuk & Tomforde.

The core of my PhD: turning a half-complete, real-time patient record into a ranked list of what is likely wrong and what to do next.

The context. In Germany’s TeleNotArzt system a physician joins an ambulance call remotely and supports the paramedics on scene. They have to judge the situation, decide on treatment, and say which measurements to take — often within a minute or two, from information that is still arriving and never complete.

What I did. I built a deep model that reads three kinds of data at once: time-series vital signs (heart rate, blood pressure over time), tabular clinical fields (age, known conditions, first observations), and free-text notes typed during the call. Each stream is encoded in its own way and then combined, so the model can use whatever is available at that moment. It returns three ranked shortlists — the most probable diagnoses, the treatments worth considering, and the measurements that would most reduce the remaining uncertainty — rather than a single hard answer.

How it is judged. Ranked suggestions need ranked metrics. I used recall@k (is the correct item in the top k suggestions?), macro- and weighted AUC (does it separate classes well, including the rare ones?), and a coverage ratio that checks the recommendations are complete — that nothing important is missing — not merely accurate.

Why it matters. A shortlist a clinician can scan and accept or reject fits the reality of emergency work better than a black-box verdict, and it degrades gracefully when data is missing. This is the system the other four papers feed into.

In short: one multimodal model, three ranked answers — likely diagnoses, candidate treatments, and what to measure next.

Reference: Kabir, M. F., Piliuk, K., & Tomforde, S. (2026). Diagnosis and treatment support as AI-driven assistant system for TeleNotary emergency care. In Proc. 18th International Conference on Agents and Artificial Intelligence — Vol. 5: ICAART, INSTICC, SciTePress, pp. 4651–4660. DOI: 10.5220/0014481000004052.

2025ICAARTGenerative models

WLSTM-GAN: generating realistic medical tables

Synthetic Data Generation for Emergency Medical Systems: A Systematic Comparison of Tabular GAN Extensions — Kabir, Nayem & Tomforde.

If real patient tables cannot be shared, can we build a stand-in that behaves like the real thing without exposing anyone’s record?

The problem. Every project above needs data, and real emergency tables are both scarce and legally hard to move between institutions. A generative model can produce artificial rows — but medical tables are awkward: they mix continuous values (blood pressure, age) with categorical ones (symptom present or absent, sex, transport type), and many table generators handle that mixture badly, blurring categories or dropping rare values.

What I did. I designed WLSTM-GAN, a GAN with a split architecture: one generator containing two specialised LSTM sub-networks, paired with two separate discriminators — one that judges the continuous columns and one that judges the categorical columns. Each data type is therefore learned and critiqued on its own terms. I then ran a systematic comparison against other tabular-GAN variants on three medical datasets, scoring each on fidelity (does the synthetic data reproduce the real statistics and relationships?) and on privacy (can a real record be recovered from the synthetic set?), both with and without an explicit privacy constraint.

What came out. WLSTM-GAN produced data that was closer to the real distribution and harder to trace back to individuals than the baseline generators — the split design pays off precisely because it stops treating a medical table as one uniform block of numbers.

Why it matters. It is the synthetic-data engine the classification and assistant-system work rely on, and a route to sharing emergency-medicine data for research without sharing patients.

In short: give the continuous and categorical columns their own LSTM generator and discriminator, and the synthetic tables come out both more realistic and more private.

Reference: Kabir, M. F., Nayem, M. M. I., & Tomforde, S. (2025). Synthetic data generation for emergency medical systems: a systematic comparison of tabular GAN extensions. In Proc. 17th International Conference on Agents and Artificial Intelligence — Vol. 3: ICAART, INSTICC, SciTePress, pp. 1199–1206. DOI: 10.5220/0013307200003890.

2024ICAARTData quality

Filling in missing values in emergency data

A Deep Analysis for Medical Emergency Missing Value Imputation — Kabir & Tomforde.

Before any model can learn from emergency data, someone has to decide what goes in the empty cells — and that choice changes the results.

The problem. The TeleNotArzt dataset is missing far more values than a typical medical dataset, and the gaps are scattered essentially at random (a measurement simply was not taken in time) rather than following a pattern a model could exploit. On top of that the data is strongly imbalanced and full of outliers from genuine rare emergencies. A careless fill-in — replacing every gap with a column average, say — erases exactly the unusual signals that matter most.

What I did. I compared a range of options for reconstructing the missing numeric values: standard statistical and machine-learning imputers, a denoising autoencoder, and a denoising LSTM autoencoder (which can use the order and timing of measurements, not just their values). All were tested on the real, non-public emergency dataset and judged on how closely the reconstructed values matched held-out ground truth.

What came out. The denoising LSTM autoencoder was consistently the most accurate, because it learns the temporal shape of a patient’s measurements rather than treating each value in isolation — and it held up better on the outlier cases.

Why it matters. Imputation is the first step in the pipeline; every later model inherits its errors. Getting it right, especially for rare cases, is a prerequisite for a trustworthy assistant.

In short: for time-stamped emergency measurements, a denoising LSTM autoencoder fills the gaps better than conventional imputation.

Reference: Kabir, M. F., & Tomforde, S. (2024). A deep analysis for medical emergency missing value imputation. In Proc. 16th International Conference on Agents and Artificial Intelligence — Vol. 3: ICAART, INSTICC, SciTePress, pp. 1229–1236. DOI: 10.5220/0012457300003636.

2024Book chapterOrganic Computing

Deep learning for highly heterogeneous emergency data

A Deep Learning Approach for the Classification of Highly Heterogeneous Data in the Context of Treatment of Medical Emergencies — Aust, …, Kabir et al. (Organic Computing, ch. 10).

The starting question: can one deep-learning pipeline make sense of emergency records where every case looks structurally different?

The problem. A single emergency record can contain numbers, categories, timestamps, and text, recorded by different people to different standards, with large parts absent. Classical models expect tidy, uniform inputs and struggle with this kind of variety.

What I did. As part of a multi-author chapter from the Organic Computing programme, I contributed to a deep-learning pipeline that ingests this heterogeneous data and classifies several targets at once — diagnostic categories, medications, and physiological measurements — so that a TeleNotArzt receives structured suggestions faster.

Why it matters. This was the proof of concept: it showed the heterogeneity of emergency data is workable, and it set up the more focused papers on data quality, synthetic data, and the full assistant system.

In short: the first step — one pipeline that classifies diagnoses, medications, and measurements straight from messy emergency records.

Reference: Aust, T., Cui, H., Botache, D., Decke, J., Frost, E., Gerling, V., Heider, M., Henrichs, E., Hubert, A., Kabir, M. F., Kisselbach, T., Meitz, L., Pilar von Pilchau, W., Oguz Saltık, A., Schönberner, C., Spils, M., Steinberger, M., Kaul, N.-H., & Reichert, H. (2024). A deep learning approach for the classification of highly heterogeneous data in the context of treatment of medical emergencies. In S. Tomforde & C. Krupitzer (Eds.), Organic Computing, ch. 10, kassel university press, pp. 121–135. DOI: 10.17170/kobra-202402269661.

Part 2

Other papers I build on

Short notes on the external work that my methods extend or compare against. These are my paraphrase for quick reference — follow the link for the authoritative version.

2019NeurIPSTabular GAN

CTGAN — Modeling Tabular Data using Conditional GAN

Xu, Skoularidou, Cuesta-Infante & Veeramachaneni.

CTGAN targets the two things that make tabular data hard for GANs: continuous columns that are multi-modal (several peaks, not one bell curve) and categorical columns that are heavily imbalanced. It applies mode-specific normalisation — fitting a variational Gaussian mixture to each numeric column and encoding a value as (which mode, how far into it) — and a conditional generator trained with training-by-sampling, which forces the model to see rare categories often enough to learn them.

Why it is on my list: it is the standard baseline for the WLSTM-GAN comparison, and the mode-normalisation trick is a direct alternative to my per-type discriminators.

Reference: Xu, L., Skoularidou, M., Cuesta-Infante, A., & Veeramachaneni, K. (2019). Modeling tabular data using conditional GAN. In Advances in Neural Information Processing Systems (NeurIPS) 32. arXiv:1907.00503.

arXiv:1907.00503 →

2018ICMLImputation

GAIN — Missing Data Imputation using GANs

Yoon, Jordon & van der Schaar.

GAIN casts imputation as a GAN. The generator fills the missing entries of a row; the discriminator then tries to say, cell by cell, which entries were real and which were generated. A hint mechanism reveals part of the mask to the discriminator, which (the authors show) is what pins the generator to the true data distribution rather than any plausible-looking fill.

Why it is on my list: it is the generative counterpoint to the autoencoder imputers in my 2024 imputation study — useful as a comparison and as a design idea for handling categorical gaps.

Reference: Yoon, J., Jordon, J., & van der Schaar, M. (2018). GAIN: missing data imputation using generative adversarial nets. In Proc. 35th International Conference on Machine Learning (ICML). arXiv:1806.02920.

arXiv:1806.02920 →

2008ICMLRepresentation learning

Denoising Autoencoders

Vincent, Larochelle, Bengio & Manzagol — “Extracting and Composing Robust Features with Denoising Autoencoders”.

An ordinary autoencoder learns to copy its input through a bottleneck. A denoising autoencoder is trained to reconstruct the clean input from a deliberately corrupted copy (values zeroed, noise added). To do that it has to learn the structure of the data, not just an identity map — which makes the learned features more robust and, conveniently, makes the model a natural imputer: treat a missing value as corruption and let the network reconstruct it.

Why it is on my list: it is the direct theoretical basis for the denoising-autoencoder and denoising-LSTM-autoencoder methods in my imputation work.

Reference: Vincent, P., Larochelle, H., Bengio, Y., & Manzagol, P.-A. (2008). Extracting and composing robust features with denoising autoencoders. In Proc. 25th International Conference on Machine Learning (ICML), pp. 1096–1103.

2017NeurIPSTransformers

Attention Is All You Need

Vaswani, Shazeer, Parmar, Uszkoreit, Jones, Gomez, Kaiser & Polosukhin.

The paper that introduced the Transformer. It drops recurrence and convolution entirely and builds sequence models from self-attention alone: every position looks at every other position and takes a weighted average, with the weights learned from the content. Add multi-head attention, positional encodings (since attention has no built-in order), and feed-forward blocks, and you get a model that trains in parallel and captures long-range dependencies cheaply.

Why it is on my list: the free-text branch of the TeleNotArzt model uses a transformer-style text encoder; understanding attention is needed to reason about how the text and signal streams are fused.

Reference: Vaswani, A., Shazeer, N., Parmar, N., Uszkoreit, J., Jones, L., Gomez, A. N., Kaiser, Ł., & Polosukhin, I. (2017). Attention is all you need. In Advances in Neural Information Processing Systems (NeurIPS) 30. arXiv:1706.03762.

arXiv:1706.03762 →

2023ICMLDiffusion

TabDDPM — Modelling Tabular Data with Diffusion Models

Kotelnikov, Baranchuk, Rubachev & Babenko.

TabDDPM brings denoising diffusion — the family of models behind modern image generators — to mixed-type tables. Numerical columns get Gaussian diffusion, categorical columns get multinomial diffusion, and a single network learns to reverse the noising process. On many benchmarks it produces synthetic data whose downstream machine-learning utility beats GAN-based synthesizers.

Why it is on my list: it is the strongest non-GAN alternative to WLSTM-GAN and a candidate for future comparison work.

Reference: Kotelnikov, A., Baranchuk, D., Rubachev, I., & Babenko, A. (2023). TabDDPM: modelling tabular data with diffusion models. In Proc. 40th International Conference on Machine Learning (ICML). arXiv:2209.15421.

Part 3

Algorithms, explained

The building blocks used across my work, each in a paragraph or two — enough to follow the papers, not a textbook.

Decision trees & gradient boosting

A decision tree splits the data with a sequence of yes/no questions (“heart rate > 120?”) until each leaf holds one prediction. Single trees are easy to read but unstable. Gradient boosting (XGBoost, LightGBM) builds many small trees in sequence, where each new tree is fit to the errors the current ensemble still makes. The result is one of the strongest models for tabular data and a common baseline in my classification experiments.

References: Breiman et al., Classification and Regression Trees (Wadsworth, 1984); Friedman, “Greedy function approximation: a gradient boosting machine,” Annals of Statistics 29(5), 2001; Chen & Guestrin, “XGBoost,” KDD 2016 — arXiv:1603.02754.

Neural networks (MLP) & backpropagation

A multilayer perceptron stacks layers of the form output = activation(W·input + b). The non-linear activation (ReLU, etc.) is what lets stacked layers represent complex functions. Training means measuring a loss, then using backpropagation — the chain rule applied layer by layer — to get the gradient of that loss with respect to every weight, and nudging the weights against it.

References: Rumelhart, Hinton & Williams, “Learning representations by back-propagating errors,” Nature 323, 1986; Goodfellow, Bengio & Courville, Deep Learning (MIT Press, 2016), ch. 6 — deeplearningbook.org.

LSTM networks

A recurrent network reads a sequence one step at a time, carrying a hidden state forward. Plain RNNs forget quickly because gradients shrink over many steps. An LSTM adds a separate cell state and three gates (input, forget, output) that learn what to keep, overwrite, or expose. That makes it good at the timing and trends in vital-sign streams, which is why it appears in both my imputation and assistant-system work.

References: Hochreiter & Schmidhuber, “Long short-term memory,” Neural Computation 9(8), 1997 — doi:10.1162/neco.1997.9.8.1735.

Autoencoders & denoising autoencoders

An autoencoder compresses an input to a small code and reconstructs it, learning the data’s structure in the bottleneck. A denoising autoencoder is trained to rebuild a clean input from a corrupted one, which makes its features more robust and turns it into a natural imputer: mark missing values as corruption and read off the reconstruction.

References: Vincent, Larochelle, Bengio & Manzagol, “Extracting and composing robust features with denoising autoencoders,” ICML 2008; Vincent et al., “Stacked denoising autoencoders,” JMLR 11, 2010 — jmlr.org/papers/v11/vincent10a.

Generative adversarial networks (GANs)

Two networks play a game. The generator turns random noise into fake samples; the discriminator tries to tell fake from real. Training the two together pushes the generator toward samples the discriminator cannot distinguish from the real distribution. GANs are powerful but delicate — unstable training and mode collapse (the generator producing only a few kinds of sample) are the usual failure modes, and much of tabular-GAN research is about avoiding them.

References: Goodfellow et al., “Generative adversarial nets,” NeurIPS 2014 — arXiv:1406.2661; Salimans et al., “Improved techniques for training GANs,” NeurIPS 2016 — arXiv:1606.03498.

Attention & transformers

Attention lets a model, for each position in a sequence, take a weighted average of all other positions, with the weights computed from how relevant each one is. Stacking multi-head attention with feed-forward layers and positional encodings gives the transformer — parallel to train, good at long-range structure, and the standard encoder for the free-text part of a multimodal clinical model.

References: Bahdanau, Cho & Bengio, “Neural machine translation by jointly learning to align and translate,” ICLR 2015 — arXiv:1409.0473; Vaswani et al., “Attention is all you need,” NeurIPS 2017 — arXiv:1706.03762.

Multimodal fusion

When inputs come in different forms — signals, tables, text — you can combine them early (concatenate raw features), late (train one model per modality and merge predictions), or jointly (encode each modality, then fuse the encodings and train end to end). The TeleNotArzt model uses joint fusion so that a weak or missing modality is down-weighted rather than breaking the pipeline.

References: Baltrušaitis, Ahuja & Morency, “Multimodal machine learning: a survey and taxonomy,” IEEE TPAMI 41(2), 2019 — arXiv:1705.09406.

Ranking metrics (recall@k, coverage)

When a model returns a ranked shortlist rather than one label, accuracy is the wrong lens. recall@k asks whether the correct item is in the top k. coverage asks whether, across a case, every action that should have been suggested was suggested somewhere in the lists. Together they measure both “is the answer near the top” and “did we miss anything”.

References: Manning, Raghavan & Schütze, Introduction to Information Retrieval (Cambridge, 2008), ch. 8 — nlp.stanford.edu/IR-book. Coverage as used here is defined in my TeleNotArzt paper — doi:10.5220/0014481000004052.

Part 4

Mathematical foundations

The handful of equations that keep coming up, with a one-line reading of each. Notation: Σ is a sum, E[·] an expected value, log the natural logarithm.

Softmax

softmax(z)_i = exp(z_i) / Σ_j exp(z_j)

Turns a vector of raw scores z into positive numbers that sum to 1 — a probability distribution over classes. The exponential exaggerates differences, so the largest score dominates.

References: Bishop, Pattern Recognition and Machine Learning (Springer, 2006), §4.3.4; Goodfellow et al., Deep Learning (2016), §6.2.2.

Cross-entropy loss

L = − Σ_c y_c · log(ŷ_c) (multi-class) L = −[ y·log(p) + (1−y)·log(1−p) ] (binary)

Penalises a prediction by how much probability it put on the wrong answer. If the model is confident and right the loss is near 0; confident and wrong sends it toward infinity. This is the loss behind almost every classifier here.

References: Goodfellow et al., Deep Learning (2016), §3.13 and §5.5; Bishop, PRML (2006), §4.3.4.

Gradient descent

θ ← θ − η · ∇θ L(θ)

Repeatedly step the parameters θ a small amount (η, the learning rate) in the direction that most decreases the loss. Backpropagation is just how ∇θ L is computed for a neural network.

References: Cauchy, “Méthode générale pour la résolution des systèmes d’équations simultanées” (1847); Ruder, “An overview of gradient descent optimization algorithms,” 2016 — arXiv:1609.04747.

The GAN objective

min_G max_D Ex∼p_data[ log D(x) ] + Ez∼p_z[ log(1 − D(G(z))) ]

The discriminator D wants to output 1 on real data and 0 on generated data; the generator G wants to make D(G(z)) close to 1. At the ideal solution the generator’s distribution matches the data and D can only guess.

References: Goodfellow et al., “Generative adversarial nets,” NeurIPS 2014 — arXiv:1406.2661.

KL and Jensen–Shannon divergence

D_KL(P ‖ Q) = Σ_x P(x) · log( P(x) / Q(x) ) JSD(P ‖ Q) = ½ D_KL(P ‖ M) + ½ D_KL(Q ‖ M), M = ½(P + Q)

Both measure how far apart two probability distributions are. KL is asymmetric and unbounded; JS is symmetric and bounded, and the original GAN objective is (up to a constant) minimising the JS divergence between the real and generated data. These are also the natural way to score how faithful synthetic data is.

References: Kullback & Leibler, “On information and sufficiency,” Annals of Mathematical Statistics 22(1), 1951; Lin, “Divergence measures based on the Shannon entropy,” IEEE Trans. Information Theory 37(1), 1991.

ROC curve and AUC

AUC = P( score(x⁺) > score(x⁻) )

Sweep the decision threshold and plot true-positive rate against false-positive rate — that is the ROC curve. The area under it (AUC) has a clean reading: the probability that the model gives a randomly chosen positive case a higher score than a randomly chosen negative one. 0.5 is chance, 1.0 is perfect. Macro AUC averages this over classes equally; weighted AUC weights by class size.

References: Hanley & McNeil, “The meaning and use of the area under a ROC curve,” Radiology 143(1), 1982; Fawcett, “An introduction to ROC analysis,” Pattern Recognition Letters 27(8), 2006.

recall@k and precision@k

recall@k = | relevant ∩ top-k | / | relevant | precision@k = | relevant ∩ top-k | / k

For a ranked shortlist of length k: recall@k is the share of the things that should have been found that appear in the top k; precision@k is the share of the top k that are actually relevant. Raising k trades precision for recall.

References: Manning, Raghavan & Schütze, Introduction to Information Retrieval (Cambridge, 2008), ch. 8 — nlp.stanford.edu/IR-book.

Part 5

Glossary

Quick definitions for terms used above and in the papers.

MCAR / MAR / MNAR
Missing Completely At Random (the gap is unrelated to anything), Missing At Random (related only to observed values), Missing Not At Random (related to the missing value itself). The emergency dataset is largely MCAR, which makes imputation harder to exploit but fairer to evaluate.
Class imbalance
Some labels appear far more often than others. A model can score high overall while being useless on the rare classes — hence macro-averaged metrics.
Fidelity (synthetic data)
How well synthetic data reproduces the real data’s distributions and the relationships between columns.
Membership-inference / privacy risk
Whether an attacker can tell that a specific real record was in the training set — the main privacy test for a generative model.
Embedding
A learned vector representation of something discrete (a word, a category) so that similar items sit near each other.
Latent space
The compressed internal representation a model works in — e.g. an autoencoder’s bottleneck, or a GAN’s noise input.
Epoch / batch
One epoch is a full pass over the training data; a batch is the small chunk processed before each weight update.
Overfitting / regularisation
Overfitting is learning the training set’s noise instead of its signal; regularisation (dropout, weight decay, early stopping) is any technique that discourages it.
Mode collapse
A GAN failure where the generator produces only a narrow slice of the real variety.
Coverage ratio
In this work, the fraction of the actions that should have been recommended for a case that actually appear somewhere in the model’s ranked lists.

References: missing-data taxonomy — Rubin, “Inference and missing data,” Biometrika 63(3), 1976; membership inference — Shokri et al., IEEE S&P 2017, arXiv:1610.05820; mode collapse — Salimans et al., NeurIPS 2016, arXiv:1606.03498; embedding / latent space / epoch / regularisation — Goodfellow, Bengio & Courville, Deep Learning (2016); coverage ratio — my TeleNotArzt paper, doi:10.5220/0014481000004052.
